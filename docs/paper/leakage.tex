\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Cloud-resource attribution --- inferring which team or service owns each
resource in a mostly-untagged cloud account --- is increasingly posed as a
graph node-classification problem, with reported accuracies in the 85--97\%
range on public traces. We show that these numbers are substantially inflated
by \emph{structural label leakage}: metadata columns routinely used as graph
edges (deployment identifiers, user identifiers, collection identifiers) are
near-deterministic functions of the attribution target, so label propagation
along them reduces the ``prediction'' to a database join. We formalize the
effect with a determinism measure and prove that label-propagation accuracy is
lower-bounded by --- and empirically tracks --- the determinism of the edge,
independent of any learning. We then audit \textbf{three cluster traces from
three vendors}. On Microsoft's 2.6M-VM Azure trace, \code{deployment\_id} maps
one-to-one to the target subscription across all 33{,}205 deployments, and
label propagation scores 97\%; removing the edge drops GraphSAGE to
\textbf{6.9\%} on 100-class attribution. On the Microsoft Philly GPU trace,
\code{user\_id} is 94.6\% deterministic and GraphSAGE falls 34\%$\rightarrow$11\%.
On Google's Borg trace, \code{collection\_id} is 100\% deterministic of
scheduling priority and label propagation falls 83\%$\rightarrow$28\%. We apply
the audit reflexively to our own synthetic benchmark (which it also flags, by
construction), release honest post-audit baselines, a reproducible harness, and
a \code{pip}-installable implementation. Our claim, stated to be falsifiable:
prior cloud-attribution results have likely been measuring leakage, not
learning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Public cloud accounts accumulate resources faster than they accumulate
governance. On a typical account, 40--60\% of spend is \emph{untagged}: the
line item exists, the dollars are real, but no metadata says which team owns
it. Every downstream cost tool --- CloudHealth, Vantage, Kubecost, native Cost
Allocation Tags --- reads tags, so untagged spend collapses into a single
opaque bucket. \emph{Cloud-resource attribution} is the task of recovering the
missing owner from behavioral signals: API-call patterns, access patterns,
cost time-series shape, network or scheduling structure.

Because resources and their interactions form a natural graph, attribution is
attractive to pose as graph node classification, and graph neural networks
(GNNs) such as GraphSAGE~\citep{hamilton2017graphsage} are a natural fit.
Reported accuracies on published cloud traces are high --- frequently
85--97\% --- which, on problems with tens to hundreds of classes, should
invite suspicion rather than celebration: state-of-the-art results on much
easier supervised problems rarely clear 95\%.

This paper argues, and demonstrates on three independent traces, that the high
numbers are largely an artifact of \textbf{structural label leakage}. Cloud
datasets ship rich structural metadata --- deployment, request, user, and
collection identifiers --- and it is natural to wire these in as graph edges.
But these columns are not independent behavioral signals; they are
\emph{near-deterministic functions of the attribution target}, because of how
organizations provision cloud resources. A deployment is scoped to one
subscription; a user submits to one cluster; a service runs at one priority.
When such a column is used as a graph edge, label propagation walks the label
along an edge that already encodes it. The model is not learning behavior; it
is performing a join.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We formalize structural leakage with a \emph{determinism} measure and
  prove that label-propagation accuracy is lower-bounded by edge determinism,
  independent of learning; a controlled experiment confirms accuracy tracks
  determinism almost exactly (Section~\ref{sec:method}, Figure~\ref{fig:sweep}).
  \item We audit \textbf{three cluster traces from three vendors} --- Microsoft
  Azure, Microsoft Philly, and Google Borg --- and report honest post-audit
  baselines. Removing one edge collapses the leading method from 97\% to 6.9\%
  (Azure), 34\% to 11\% (Philly), and 83\% to 28\% (Google)
  (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item We apply the audit \emph{reflexively} to our own synthetic benchmark,
  which it also flags, and disclose rather than special-plead
  (Section~\ref{sec:reflexive}).
  \item We release a reproducible harness, per-dataset benchmark scripts, and a
  \code{pip}-installable implementation (\code{leakaudit}) so the check is one
  line in any pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:repro}).
\end{enumerate}

We state our thesis as a falsifiable claim: \emph{across published
cloud-attribution benchmarks, the dominant predictive signal is structural
metadata that deterministically encodes the target, and honest behavioral
attribution is much weaker than the literature suggests.} We would be glad to
be shown a counterexample (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Background and Related Work}

\paragraph{Label / target leakage.} Leakage --- information about the target
entering features in a way unavailable at prediction time, or trivially
encoding the label --- is a well-known failure mode in applied machine
learning~\citep{kaufman2012leakage}. Our contribution is not the concept but
its specific, systematic form in cloud-attribution \emph{graphs}, where the
leak enters through edge construction rather than a feature column, together
with a mechanical audit and a formal characterization.

\paragraph{Shortcut learning.} Models frequently exploit spurious but
predictive shortcuts rather than the intended
signal~\citep{geirhos2020shortcut}. Structural leakage is a shortcut with an
unusually clean signature: the shortcut edge is a near-perfect lookup table, so
the effect is not subtle degradation under distribution shift but an inflated
in-distribution number that collapses the instant the edge is removed.

\paragraph{Graph learning and baselines.} We evaluate GraphSAGE
\citep{hamilton2017graphsage} against label propagation
\citep{zhu2002labelprop}, a \code{node2vec} embedding
\citep{grover2016node2vec} fed to logistic regression, and feature-only
baselines. Label propagation is the sharpest diagnostic: with no learned
parameters and no features, it simply spreads labels across edges, so a leaking
edge sends it straight to a near-perfect score. A GNN inherits a weaker form of
the same effect, because message passing over a within-class edge pulls
same-class nodes together in embedding space.

\paragraph{Datasets.} The Azure Public Dataset derives from Microsoft's
Resource Central~\citep{cortez2017resourcecentral}; the Philly traces are the
Microsoft GPU-cluster study of \citet{jeon2019philly}; the Borg trace is
Google's cluster-usage release~\citep{tirmazi2020borg}. All three were released
for systems research (workload prediction, scheduling), not attribution --- our
use is off-label, and the leakage we report is a property of that off-label
use, not a defect in the datasets.

\paragraph{Calibration.} Where we report confidence, it is calibrated post-hoc
via temperature scaling~\citep{guo2017calibration}.

\section{The Determinism Audit}
\label{sec:method}

Let a dataset be a table with a target column $t$ (the attribution label) and a
set of candidate columns $\{c\}$ that could be used as graph edges. For a
candidate $c$, partition the rows by the value of $c$ and, within each group,
count the distinct target values. Define the \emph{determinism} of $c$ with
respect to $t$ as the fraction of $c$-groups that map to exactly one target
value:
\[
  D(c, t) \;=\;
  \frac{\bigl|\{\, v \in \mathrm{vals}(c) \;:\; |\{\, t(r) : c(r) = v \,\}| = 1 \,\}\bigr|}
       {|\mathrm{vals}(c)|}.
\]
$D(c,t)=1$ means every value of $c$ maps to a single target value: $c$ is a
perfect lookup of $t$. In code, the audit is two lines of \code{pandas} per
candidate:
\begin{quote}
\begin{verbatim}
for c in candidate_edge_cols:
    determinism = (df.groupby(c)[target].nunique() == 1).mean()
    if determinism >= threshold:      # flag c as leaking
        print(c, determinism)
\end{verbatim}
\end{quote}
We use a default threshold of $0.85$. Two caveats matter. \emph{(i) High
cardinality.} A column with as many distinct values as rows (a primary key) is
trivially deterministic; we flag such columns separately, as their determinism
is a cardinality artifact rather than a structural leak, though both should be
excluded from the graph. \emph{(ii) Coverage.} The audit only checks the columns
it is given; a clean report is evidence about those columns, not a guarantee.

\subsection{Why determinism inflates accuracy}

Determinism is not merely correlated with inflated accuracy; for label
propagation it \emph{lower-bounds} it.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:bound}
Let $G$ be the graph whose connected components are the edge-groups of a
candidate column $c$ (nodes sharing a value of $c$ are connected), with minimum
group size $k$, and let each node carry a training label independently with
probability $\rho$. Under majority-vote label propagation, the expected test
accuracy satisfies
\[
  \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{acc}] \;\ge\; D(c,t)\,\bigl(1-(1-\rho)^{k-1}\bigr).
\]
In particular, for well-covered graphs $\mathrm{acc}\approx D(c,t)$: a perfectly
deterministic edge ($D=1$) yields near-perfect accuracy with no learning.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Consider a test node $u$ whose group is \emph{pure} (all members share $u$'s
class). If $u$ has at least one labeled neighbor, majority vote returns that
shared class, so $u$ is classified correctly. The probability $u$ has a labeled
neighbor is $1-(1-\rho)^{k-1}$. Pure groups constitute a $D(c,t)$ fraction of
groups by definition, and their nodes are correctly classified with at least
that probability; mixed groups contribute a nonnegative remainder, which we
drop for the bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{fig-sweep.pdf}
\caption{Label-propagation accuracy is a function of edge determinism, not model
quality. We inject a single edge at controlled determinism into a 5-class task
(400 nodes, $\rho=0.7$, group size 4) and measure label-propagation test
accuracy (mean $\pm$ std over 5 seeds). Accuracy tracks determinism almost
exactly, matching Proposition~\ref{prop:bound} (at $D{=}0.99$ the bound predicts
$96.3\%$; empirical is $96.0\%$). Below $D\approx0.2$ a mixed edge \emph{actively
misleads} propagation, dropping accuracy below the random floor.}
\label{fig:sweep}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:sweep} confirms the proposition empirically: with the label the
\emph{only} signal (node features are pure noise), label-propagation accuracy
rises near-linearly with the injected edge's determinism, from below random to
$96\%$. This is the whole mechanism in one curve --- ``accuracy'' on a leaking
graph is a readout of edge determinism, and a deterministic edge requires no
learning at all.

\section{Datasets and Setup}

\paragraph{Azure Public Dataset.} 2.6M virtual machines across 100 subscriptions
over 30 days~\citep{cortez2017resourcecentral}; target is the subscription. The
trace ships \emph{summary} CPU statistics per VM (max / avg / p95), not the full
hourly series, so per-resource features are thin.

\paragraph{Philly GPU-cluster trace.} 117K deep-learning training jobs across
virtual clusters~\citep{jeon2019philly}; target is the virtual cluster. We
sample the top-15 clusters ($\le 200$ jobs each; 2{,}317 jobs).

\paragraph{Google Borg trace.} Google's cluster-usage trace of instance
events~\citep{tirmazi2020borg}. Borg has no ``team,'' but it has the same
structural shape: we predict an instance's \emph{scheduling priority} (5 tiers)
from its resource-request behavior, balanced to $\le 400$ instances per tier.

\paragraph{Synthetic environment.} A controlled generator with configurable
class count and injected hard cases, used for the mechanism experiment and for
ablations where the number of labels can be controlled.

\paragraph{Models and protocol.} GraphSAGE against Majority, logistic
regression, $k$-NN ($k{=}5$), label propagation, and \code{node2vec}$+$LR.
Accuracy is a stratified train/test split (70/30, 80 epochs) averaged over
random seeds. Random-chance accuracy is $1/N$ for $N$ classes.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Azure: a 100\%-deterministic edge}

The audit flags \code{deployment\_id} at determinism $1.000$: across all
33{,}205 deployments, every deployment belongs to exactly one subscription.
Wired in as a graph edge, it drives label propagation to a 97\% ``result'' on
100-class attribution --- a graph-database join. Removing the leaking edges and
re-running gives the honest picture in Table~\ref{tab:azure}: GraphSAGE at 100
classes scores $6.9\pm0.5$, roughly $7\times$ the 1\% random floor and ahead of
every baseline, but an order of magnitude below the leaked headline.

\subsection{Philly: a partial leak, same story}

On a structurally unrelated dataset --- different domain, team, and target ---
the audit flags \code{user\_id} at determinism $0.946$: ${\sim}95\%$ of users
submit to exactly one cluster. The honest, non-leaking machine edge sits at
$0.144$. Removing the user edge drops GraphSAGE from 34\% to 11\% at 15 clusters
(Table~\ref{tab:philly}). A methodologically important detail: on the
\emph{de-leaked} graph, GraphSAGE does not win --- label propagation
($17.6\pm1.5$) and \code{node2vec}$+$LR ($13.4\pm0.6$) both edge out GraphSAGE
($11.3\pm1.0$). We report this because it is true and because it sharpens the
point: once the shortcut is gone, \emph{every} method collapses to the low
teens.

\subsection{Google Borg: a third vendor, same signature}

The audit flags \code{collection\_id} at determinism $1.000$ of scheduling

\subsection{The audit turned on ourselves}
\label{sec:reflexive}

A methodology paper that audits others must audit itself. Running the
determinism check on our own synthetic benchmark flags \code{vpc\_cidr} and
\code{iam\_role} at determinism $1.000$ --- the identical signature we call a
leak on Azure. This is \emph{by construction}: the generator models real
organizational structure (each team owns a VPC; role names follow team
conventions), and those columns are used as edges. We disclose rather than
special-plead. The audit alone cannot distinguish a benchmark artifact (Azure:
exclude it) from deliberately modeled world-structure (here: it is the thing
being simulated); by the audit's own standard, both trigger. Two facts keep the
synthetic results meaningful: an edge-dropout ablation shows $89.4\%$ accuracy
with the graph \emph{entirely removed}, so the headline does not depend on the
deterministic edges; and we label synthetic results as ablations. The
attribution tool now runs this audit on its own inputs before training and
warns when any column deterministically encodes the label.

\subsection{A real, non-leaking validation}

To check that behavioral attribution works \emph{at all} when features are rich,
we provisioned a real AWS account with full CloudTrail via
infrastructure-as-code, hand-labeled 15 resources across three teams, and ran
end to end: $13/15 = 87\%$ correct (95\% Wilson interval $[62\%,96\%]$), and
$13/14 = 93\%$ among high-confidence predictions ($[69\%,99\%]$; the two errors
both fell below the confidence threshold and were routed to human review, as
designed). This validates the engineering pipeline, not the methodology --- with
15 labels the interval is wide --- but it demonstrates that the collapse on
public traces is a property of \emph{thin features plus structural leakage}, not
of the approach.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why this generalizes.} Structural leakage is not a vendor bug; it is
a property of how cloud resources are provisioned. Teams create resources inside
deployments, submit jobs as users, and run services as collections --- and each
of those units is scoped to one subscription, cluster, or priority. The
structural metadata therefore \emph{reflects the org chart}, and the org chart
is the attribution target. Any dataset generated this way will exhibit the
pattern, and any graph-attribution method that uses the metadata as edges will
exploit it. That we find the identical signature on three unrelated traces from
two companies is consistent with a structural cause, not a coincidence.

\paragraph{The falsifiable claim.} We state, to be refuted: prior published
cloud-attribution results that use deployment / request / user / collection
identifiers as graph signals report accuracies inflated by structural leakage,
and honest behavioral attribution on many-class problems is single-digit to
mid-teens percent. The claim fails if a cloud-attribution result exists that
runs the determinism audit, excludes leaking edges, and still reports high
behavioral accuracy. We have not found one.

\paragraph{A minimum reporting standard.} We propose that any cloud-attribution
result report, alongside its accuracy, the determinism of every column used as
an edge with respect to the target. It is one line of code per column and, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:bound}, it converts an unfalsifiable ``we got 95\%'' into
a checkable claim.

\section{Threats to Validity}

\paragraph{Construct validity.} Our determinism measure treats a column as a
leak if its \emph{values} map to single targets; a genuine behavioral feature
that happens to be single-use (e.g. a unique key) is flagged the same way. We
mitigate with the high-cardinality flag, but the audit is a screen, not a
proof: a flagged column warrants inspection, not automatic deletion.

\paragraph{Internal validity.} The controlled sweep uses noise features to
isolate the edge; with informative features a GNN can also succeed without the
edge, so the sweep bounds the \emph{leakage} contribution, not total accuracy.
This is intended --- it isolates the mechanism --- but the clean linear relation
of Figure~\ref{fig:sweep} is specific to label propagation.

\paragraph{External validity.} Three traces are not the field. Our thesis about
prior work is an inference from the mechanism plus three data points, not a
re-audit of specific systems; we offer it as a falsifiable hypothesis. The
Borg ``priority'' target is a proxy for ownership, chosen because Borg is
anonymized; it demonstrates the structural-leak pattern, not team attribution
per se.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Thin features on public traces.} The public traces ship summary or
request-level statistics only; the small margin of GraphSAGE over feature-only
baselines partly reflects feature poverty. We cannot quantify the rich-feature
regime on public data because no public trace we know of has both rich
per-resource behavior and no structural leak --- itself a gap worth filling.
\textbf{Small real-data label set.} The real-AWS validation has 15 labels and a
wide interval; it is engineering evidence, not a methodology result. \textbf{We
do not re-run prior work.} \textbf{Synthetic is synthetic}, and as disclosed
fails its own audit by construction.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

All results reproduce from the open-source repository. The audit is released as
a standalone, tested package:
\begin{quote}
\begin{verbatim}
pip install leakaudit
leakaudit data.csv --target subscription_id --fail-on-leak   # CI gate
\end{verbatim}
\end{quote}
Per-dataset scripts regenerate every table and the figure:
\code{bench-azure.py}, \code{bench-philly.py}, \code{bench-google.py}, and
\code{bench-determinism-sweep.py}; each emits the leaky and de-leaked
configurations in one artifact, and the reflexive synthetic audit is pinned by
a regression test. Protocol: stratified 70/30 split, 80 epochs, multi-seed with
reported standard deviations; random seeds are fixed and listed in each script.
Code, data pointers, and the harness are at
\href{https://github.com/pauti04/CostDNA}{\code{github.com/pauti04/CostDNA}}.

\section{Conclusion}

Cloud-resource attribution looks nearly solved on public benchmarks and is not.
The high numbers come from structural metadata that deterministically encodes
the target and, used as a graph edge, turns learning into a lookup --- an effect
we characterize formally and confirm on three vendors' traces, and, run
reflexively, on our own. Honest behavioral attribution is real but modest:
${\sim}7\times$ random at 100 classes on Azure, ${\sim}1.7\times$ at 15 clusters
on Philly, and at the feature floor on Borg. We release the audit, the honest
baselines, and the harness, and we ask the field to report the one number that
makes the claim checkable: the determinism of every edge against the target.
