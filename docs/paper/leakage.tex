\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Cloud-resource attribution --- inferring which team owns each resource in a
mostly-untagged cloud account --- is increasingly posed as a graph
node-classification problem, with reported accuracies in the 85--97\% range on
public traces. We show that these numbers are substantially inflated by
\emph{structural label leakage}: metadata columns routinely used as graph
edges (deployment identifiers, user identifiers, IAM principals) are
near-deterministic functions of the attribution target, so message passing
along them reduces the ``prediction'' to a database join. On Microsoft's
2.6M-VM Azure Public Dataset, a single graph edge (\code{deployment\_id}) maps
one-to-one to the target subscription across all 33{,}205 deployments;
removing it drops GraphSAGE from a headline 97\% to \textbf{6.9\%} on 100-class
attribution --- still ${\sim}7\times$ random, but far from the headline. The
same audit on the Philly GPU-cluster trace finds \code{user\_id} 94.6\%
deterministic of the virtual cluster, dropping GraphSAGE from 34\% to 11\% at
15 clusters. We propose a two-line \emph{determinism audit} as a minimum
reporting standard, apply it reflexively to our own synthetic benchmark
(which it also flags, by construction), and release honest post-audit
baselines, a reproducible harness, and a \code{pip}-installable implementation.
Our central claim, stated to be falsifiable: prior cloud-attribution results
have likely been measuring leakage rather than learning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Public cloud accounts accumulate resources faster than they accumulate
governance. On a typical account, 40--60\% of spend is \emph{untagged}: the
line item exists, the dollars are real, but no metadata says which team owns
it. Every downstream cost tool --- CloudHealth, Vantage, Kubecost, native Cost
Allocation Tags --- reads tags, so untagged spend collapses into a single
opaque bucket. \emph{Cloud-resource attribution} is the task of recovering the
missing owner: given behavioral signals (API-call patterns, IAM access, cost
time-series shape, network topology), predict the owning team for each
resource.

Because resources and their interactions form a natural graph, attribution is
attractive to pose as graph node classification, and graph neural networks
(GNNs) such as GraphSAGE~\citep{hamilton2017graphsage} are a natural fit.
Reported accuracies on published cloud traces are high --- frequently
85--97\% --- which, on problems with tens to hundreds of classes, should
invite suspicion rather than celebration: state-of-the-art results on much
easier supervised problems rarely clear 95\%.

This paper argues that the high numbers are largely an artifact of
\textbf{structural label leakage}. Cloud datasets ship rich structural
metadata --- deployment identifiers, request identifiers, user identifiers,
IAM role names --- and it is natural to wire these in as graph edges. But
these columns are not independent behavioral signals; they are
\emph{near-deterministic functions of the attribution target}, because of how
organizations provision cloud resources. A deployment is scoped to one
subscription; a user belongs to one team. When such a column is used as a
graph edge, message passing propagates the label along an edge that already
encodes the label. The model is not learning behavior; it is performing a
join.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We identify structural label leakage as a systematic confound in
  cloud-attribution benchmarks and give a precise, model-agnostic
  \emph{determinism audit} for detecting it (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item We apply the audit to two independent, widely-used Microsoft datasets
  --- the 2.6M-VM Azure Public Dataset and the 117K-job Philly GPU-cluster
  trace --- and report honest post-audit baselines. On Azure, GraphSAGE falls
  from a 97\% headline to \textbf{6.9\%} at 100 classes; on Philly, from 34\%
  to \textbf{11\%} at 15 clusters (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item We apply the audit \emph{reflexively} to our own synthetic benchmark,
  which it also flags, and disclose rather than special-plead
  (Section~\ref{sec:reflexive}).
  \item We release a reproducible harness, per-dataset benchmark scripts, and
  a \code{pip}-installable implementation of the audit
  (\code{leakaudit}), so the check is one line in any pipeline
  (Section~\ref{sec:repro}).
\end{enumerate}

We state our thesis as a falsifiable claim: \emph{across published
cloud-attribution benchmarks, the dominant predictive signal is structural
metadata that deterministically encodes the target, and honest behavioral
attribution is much weaker than the literature suggests.} We would be glad to
be shown a counterexample (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Background and Related Work}

\paragraph{Label / target leakage.} Leakage --- information about the target
leaking into features that would not be available at prediction time, or that
trivially encodes the label --- is a well-known failure mode in applied
machine learning~\citep{kaufman2012leakage}. Our contribution is not the
concept but its specific, systematic form in cloud-attribution \emph{graphs},
where the leak enters through edge construction rather than through a feature
column, and a mechanical audit that catches it.

\paragraph{Shortcut learning.} Models frequently exploit spurious but
predictive shortcuts rather than the intended
signal~\citep{geirhos2020shortcut}. Structural leakage is a shortcut with an
unusually clean signature: the shortcut edge is a near-perfect lookup table,
so the effect is not subtle degradation under distribution shift but an
inflated in-distribution number that collapses the moment the edge is removed.

\paragraph{Graph learning and baselines.} We evaluate GraphSAGE
\citep{hamilton2017graphsage} against label propagation
\citep{zhu2002labelprop}, a \code{node2vec} embedding
\citep{grover2016node2vec} fed to logistic regression, and standard
feature-only baselines. Label propagation is the sharpest diagnostic: with no
learned parameters, it simply spreads labels across edges, so a leaking edge
sends it straight to a near-perfect score.

\paragraph{Datasets.} The Azure Public Dataset derives from Microsoft's
Resource Central~\citep{cortez2017resourcecentral}; the Philly traces are the
Microsoft GPU-cluster workload study of \citet{jeon2019philly}. Both were
released for systems research (workload prediction, scheduling), not for
attribution --- our use is off-label, and the leakage we report is a property
of that off-label use, not a defect in the datasets.

\paragraph{Calibration.} Where we report confidence, it is calibrated
post-hoc via temperature scaling~\citep{guo2017calibration}.

\section{The Determinism Audit}
\label{sec:method}

Let a dataset be a table with a target column $t$ (the attribution label) and
a set of candidate columns $\{c\}$ that could be used as graph edges or
features. For a candidate column $c$, partition the rows by the value of $c$
and, within each group, count the distinct values of $t$. Define the
\emph{determinism} of $c$ with respect to $t$ as the fraction of $c$-groups
that map to exactly one target value:
\[
  D(c, t) \;=\;
  \frac{\bigl|\{\, v \in \mathrm{vals}(c) \;:\; |\{\, t(r) : c(r) = v \,\}| = 1 \,\}\bigr|}
       {|\mathrm{vals}(c)|}.
\]
$D(c,t) = 1$ means every value of $c$ maps to a single target value: $c$ is
a perfect lookup of $t$. Using such a $c$ as a graph edge makes label
propagation exact and gives any message-passing model the answer for free.
Values below $1$ indicate \emph{partial} leakage --- still enough, at high
values, for an edge to carry most of the signal.

In code, the audit is two lines of \code{pandas} per candidate:

\begin{quote}
\begin{verbatim}
for c in candidate_edge_cols:
    determinism = (df.groupby(c)[target].nunique() == 1).mean()
    if determinism >= threshold:      # flag c as leaking
        print(c, determinism)
\end{verbatim}
\end{quote}

We use a default threshold of $0.85$. Two caveats matter in practice.
\emph{(i) High cardinality.} A column with as many distinct values as rows
(e.g. a primary-key identifier) is trivially deterministic; we flag such
columns separately, because their determinism is a cardinality artifact rather
than a structural leak, though both should be excluded from the graph.
\emph{(ii) Coverage.} The audit only checks the columns it is given; a clean
report is evidence about those columns, not a guarantee of no leakage
elsewhere.

\section{Datasets and Setup}

\paragraph{Azure Public Dataset.} 2.6M virtual machines across 100
subscriptions over 30 days~\citep{cortez2017resourcecentral}. We treat the
subscription as the attribution target and build a resource graph from
metadata columns. The trace ships \emph{summary} CPU statistics (max / average
/ p95) per VM rather than the full hourly time series, so per-resource
features are thin --- a limitation we return to.

\paragraph{Philly GPU-cluster trace.} 117K deep-learning training jobs across
virtual clusters~\citep{jeon2019philly}. The target is the virtual cluster.
Each job carries a submitting user, the machines it ran on, GPU counts, and
timing. We sample the top-15 clusters (${\le}200$ jobs each; 2{,}317 jobs) for
the class-count sweep.

\paragraph{Synthetic environment.} A controlled generator with configurable
team count and injected hard cases (cross-team resources, reassigned
ownership) used for ablations where the number of labels can be controlled.

\paragraph{Models and protocol.} GraphSAGE against Majority, logistic
regression, $k$-NN ($k{=}5$), label propagation, and \code{node2vec}$+$LR.
Accuracy is a stratified train/test split averaged over random seeds
(Azure: multi-seed; Philly: seeds $\{7,42,911\}$; 70/30 split, 80 epochs).
Random-chance accuracy is $1/N$ for $N$ classes.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Azure: a 100\%-deterministic edge}

Running the audit on the Azure metadata immediately flags \code{deployment\_id}
at determinism $1.000$: across all 33{,}205 deployments in the trace, every
deployment belongs to exactly one subscription. Wired in as a graph edge, it
drives label propagation to a 97\% ``result'' on 100-class attribution --- a
graph-database join, not learning. Removing the leaking edges and re-running
gives the honest picture in Table~\ref{tab:azure}.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Azure post-audit test accuracy (\%), leaking edges removed. Random
$=1/N$. GraphSAGE wins consistently but the absolute numbers are modest,
because the trace ships only summary CPU statistics per VM.}
\label{tab:azure}
\begin{tabular}{rrrrrrr}
\toprule
$N$ teams & Random & Majority & LogReg & $k$-NN & LabelProp & \textbf{GraphSAGE} \\
\midrule
5   & 20.0 & 17.2 & 31.3 & 28.6 & 20.0 & \textbf{34.6} \\
10  & 10.0 &  8.4 & 18.3 & 17.3 & 10.0 & \textbf{22.5} \\
25  &  4.0 &  3.3 &  9.2 & 10.0 &  4.0 & \textbf{10.6} \\
50  &  2.0 &  1.6 &  5.8 &  6.2 &  2.0 & \textbf{9.2}  \\
100 &  1.0 &  0.7 &  3.4 &  3.8 &  1.0 & \textbf{6.9}  \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

At 100 classes GraphSAGE scores $6.9 \pm 0.5$, roughly $7\times$ the 1\% random
floor and ahead of every feature-only baseline, but an order of magnitude below
the leaked 97\% headline. The gap over feature-only baselines is small because
the features are thin; the point is not that behavioral attribution is
worthless but that its honest ceiling on this trace is single-digit percent,
not near-perfect.

\subsection{Philly: a partial leak, same story}

We ran the identical audit on a structurally unrelated dataset --- a different
domain (training jobs, not VMs), a different team, a different target. The
audit flags \code{user\_id} at determinism $0.946$: ${\sim}95\%$ of users
submit to exactly one virtual cluster. The honest, non-leaking machine edge
(\code{machine\_id}) sits at $0.144$ --- machines are shared across clusters,
exactly the signal one would \emph{want}. Table~\ref{tab:philly} shows the
before/after.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Philly: GraphSAGE with the leaking \code{user\_id} edge (``leaky'')
vs. with it removed (``de-leaked''), by cluster count. Removing one edge
collapses accuracy roughly threefold.}
\label{tab:philly}
\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}
\toprule
$N$ clusters & Random & Leaky GraphSAGE & \textbf{De-leaked GraphSAGE} & Ratio to random \\
\midrule
3  & 33.3 & 91.2 & \textbf{54.4} & $1.6\times$ \\
5  & 20.0 & 62.2 & \textbf{35.4} & $1.8\times$ \\
10 & 10.0 & 42.9 & \textbf{18.4} & $1.8\times$ \\
15 &  6.7 & 34.2 & \textbf{11.3} & $1.7\times$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

A methodologically important detail: on the \emph{de-leaked} Philly graph,
GraphSAGE does \textbf{not} win. Table~\ref{tab:philly15} gives the full sweep
at 15 clusters; label propagation ($17.6\%$) and \code{node2vec}$+$LR
($13.4\%$) both edge out GraphSAGE ($11.3\%$). We report this because it is
true and because it sharpens the thesis: once the structural shortcut is gone,
\emph{every} method collapses to the low teens. The interesting quantity is
not which model wins the honest task but how far all of them fall from the
leaked headline.

Two unrelated public datasets, the same signature: a structural-metadata
column that deterministically encodes the target and, used as an edge, does
almost all of the work.

\subsection{The audit turned on ourselves}
\label{sec:reflexive}

A methodology paper that audits others must audit itself. Running the
determinism check on our own synthetic benchmark flags \code{vpc\_cidr} and
\code{iam\_role} at determinism $1.000$ --- the identical numeric signature we
call a leak on Azure. This is \emph{by construction}: the generator models real
organizational structure (each team owns a VPC; role names follow team
conventions), and those columns are used as edges.

We disclose rather than special-plead. The audit alone cannot distinguish a
\emph{benchmark artifact} (Azure: exclude it) from \emph{deliberately modeled
world-structure} (here: it is the thing being simulated); by the audit's own
numeric standard, both trigger. Two facts keep the synthetic results
meaningful: (i) an edge-dropout ablation shows $89.4\%$ accuracy with the graph
\emph{entirely removed}, so the headline does not depend on the deterministic
edges; and (ii) we label synthetic results as ablations, not headline numbers.
The attribution tool itself now runs this audit on its own inputs before
training and warns when any input column deterministically encodes the label.

\subsection{A real, non-leaking validation}

To check that behavioral attribution works \emph{at all} when features are
rich, we provisioned a real AWS account with full CloudTrail via
infrastructure-as-code, hand-labeled 15 resources across three teams, and ran
end to end: $13/15 = 87\%$ correct overall, and $13/14 = 93\%$ among
high-confidence predictions (the two errors both fell below the confidence
threshold and were routed to human review, as designed). This validates the
engineering pipeline, not the methodology --- with 15 labels the confidence
interval is wide (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) --- but it demonstrates that
the collapse on public traces is a property of \emph{thin features plus
structural leakage}, not of the approach.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why this generalizes.} Structural leakage is not a Microsoft bug; it
is a property of how cloud resources are provisioned. Engineering teams create
resources inside deployments, and deployments are scoped to a single
subscription, project, or cluster. The structural metadata therefore
\emph{reflects the org chart}, and the org chart is exactly the attribution
target. Any dataset generated this way will exhibit the pattern, and any
graph-attribution method that uses the metadata as edges will exploit it.

\paragraph{The falsifiable claim.} We state, to be refuted: prior published
cloud-attribution results that use deployment / request / user / cluster
identifiers as graph signals report accuracies inflated by structural leakage,
and honest behavioral attribution on 100-class problems is single-digit to
mid-teens percent. The claim fails if a cloud-attribution result exists that
runs the determinism audit, excludes leaking edges, and still reports high
behavioral accuracy. We have not found one; we would like to.

\paragraph{A minimum reporting standard.} We propose that any
cloud-attribution result report, alongside its accuracy, the determinism of
every column used as an edge or feature with respect to the target. It is one
line of code per column and it converts an unfalsifiable ``we got 95\%'' into a
checkable claim.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Thin features on public traces.} The Azure trace ships summary CPU
statistics only; the small margin of GraphSAGE over feature-only baselines
partly reflects feature poverty, not a limit of graph learning. We cannot
quantify the rich-feature regime on public data because no public trace we
know of has both rich per-resource behavior and no structural leak ---
itself a gap worth filling.

\textbf{Small real-data label set.} The real-AWS validation has 15 labels;
its 87\% point estimate carries a wide interval and must not be read as a
methodology result. A production claim would need 100+ labels.

\textbf{We do not re-run prior work.} Our thesis about the literature is an
inference from two datasets plus the mechanism, not a re-audit of specific
published systems; it is offered as a falsifiable hypothesis, not a proof.

\textbf{Synthetic is synthetic.} The synthetic environment demonstrates the
rich-feature regime by construction and, as disclosed, fails its own audit by
construction; it is an ablation instrument, not evidence about real accuracy.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

All results reproduce from the open-source repository. The determinism audit is
released as a standalone, tested package:
\begin{quote}
\begin{verbatim}
pip install leakaudit
leakaudit data.csv --target subscription_id --fail-on-leak   # CI gate
\end{verbatim}
\end{quote}
Per-dataset benchmark scripts (\code{scripts/bench-azure.py},
\code{scripts/bench-philly.py}) regenerate Tables~\ref{tab:azure}--\ref{tab:philly15},
including the leaky-vs-de-leaked comparison in a single artifact; the
reflexive synthetic audit is pinned by a regression test. Code, data pointers,
and the full harness are at
\href{https://github.com/pauti04/CostDNA}{\code{github.com/pauti04/CostDNA}}.

\section{Conclusion}

Cloud-resource attribution looks nearly solved on public benchmarks and is
not. The high numbers come from structural metadata that deterministically
encodes the target and, used as a graph edge, turns learning into a lookup. A
two-line determinism audit exposes it on two independent Microsoft datasets ---
and, run reflexively, on our own. Honest behavioral attribution is real but
modest: ${\sim}7\times$ random at 100 classes on Azure, ${\sim}1.7\times$ at
15 clusters on Philly. We release the audit, the honest baselines, and the
harness, and we invite the field to report the one number that makes the claim
checkable: the determinism of every edge against the target.
